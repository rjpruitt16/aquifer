Aquifer

Keep the machines alive until reinforcements arrive.

A circuit breaker protects a backend by refusing work fast and leaving callers to retry. Aquifer holds the work, gives every tenant a turn, and releases it at the pace the machines advertise. Below: the idea you can play with, then what the benchmarks measured.

A proxy has to guess from the outside: fixed limits, or latency it measures after the damage is done. So when capacity drops, the traffic hitting it goes up, because every refusal comes back as a retry. Aquifer is told. The machines put the rate they can take on every response (X-Aqueduct-Rps), it moves up and down with their real capacity, and what Aquifer releases follows it. Drag the slider under the Aquifer side to change the rate yourself: that is the backend sending a different header, with nothing redeployed.

  
The big tenant waits longer on the right. An equal turn gives it less than the share it can grab by volume on the left. That cost is real, and the benchmarks below show it too.






  

    
Answer on the same connection for as long as it can
How it works

    
A webhook gateway usually takes the event, answers at once, and delivers later. Aquifer starts one step earlier: while the target is healthy the caller gets the real answer on the same connection, with no queue in the way. It only becomes a queue when the target says it is struggling, and the target says so with headers on its own responses.

When help comes late, how long does the unluckiest caller wait?
Real run data

    
Traffic runs at three times what the backend can do. Capacity grows from 8 workers to 32 some minutes in, the way an autoscaler catches up. Callers retry until their job is done. Both front doors finished every job. With help 10 minutes away, the longest wait was about 11 minutes behind Aquifer and 24 behind Envoy.

A retry storm, replayed
Real run data

    
Four services sit in a chain and each one retries three times, so one refusal at the bottom can become 27 calls. Fifteen tenants push up to three times what the backend can do for 40 minutes. The backend goes down for a minute, then its capacity doubles. Same backend, same load, two front doors.

One runtime for every kind of traffic
Illustration, not benchmark data

    
Webhooks arriving, agents calling tools over MCP, model calls headed to a GPU and calls to your own backend all pass through the same queue. Each target says how fast it wants work, and Aquifer releases at that pace. Send a burst, change a target's pace, then switch Aquifer off and watch the same traffic.
